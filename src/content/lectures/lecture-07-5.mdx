## The idea in one sentence

The vectors a matrix sends to zero form a flat space through the origin, and one pass of
elimination hands you a basis for it.

## Two receipts

A shop sells two items. The first costs one dollar, the second costs two. A basket is a pair of
counts $x = (x_1, x_2)$, and returns are allowed, so a count is any real number. The till computes

$$
Ax = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}
   = x_1 + 2x_2 .
$$

Two questions, same $A$, different targets. Which baskets total six dollars? Which baskets total
nothing? Both answers are infinite sets of points, and both are lines in the plane. They look like
twins.

They are not. One of those lines is a subspace and the other is not, and everything this course
does with solutions rests on that difference. Lecture 7 gave you the test: a subspace holds the
zero vector, and it survives scaling and addition. Point the test at both lines.

## Add two answers together

Two baskets that total six: $u = (6, 0)$ and $v = (0, 3)$. Check them against the till. Six items
of the first kind cost $6 + 0 = 6$. Three of the second cost $0 + 6 = 6$.

Now put both baskets into one cart. The counts add, so the combined basket is $u + v = (6, 3)$,
and the till says $6 + 6 = 12$. Twelve dollars. The sum of two six-dollar baskets is not a
six-dollar basket. Doubling fails for the same reason: $2u = (12, 0)$ costs twelve.

Two baskets that total nothing: $p = (2, -1)$, which buys two of the first item and returns one
of the second, and $q = (-6, 3)$, which returns six and buys three. The till says $2 - 2 = 0$ and $-6 + 6 = 0$.
Add them: $p + q = (-4, 2)$, and the till says $-4 + 4 = 0$. Still nothing. Scale $p$ by any
number you like and the total stays at zero, because scaling the basket scales the receipt.

## The set that misses the origin

The zero-dollar line contains the origin: the empty basket costs nothing. The six-dollar line does
not, since the empty basket does not cost six dollars. That alone disqualifies it, because every
subspace contains the zero vector.

A flat set pushed off the origin like this is called an affine set. It can have any dimension, and it is
perfectly straight. Neither of those saves it. An affine set is a subspace only when the
push is zero. The six-dollar line is the zero-dollar line shifted by any single six-dollar basket.
Shift by $u = (6,0)$ or by $v = (0,3)$ and you land on the same line.

<Callout type="note">
  Averages are the one operation an affine set survives. Take half of $u$ plus half of $v$. That
  is $(3, 1.5)$, and the till says $3 + 3 = 6$. Any weighted combination whose weights add up to
  one lands back on the six-dollar line, because the receipt of a combination is the same
  combination of the receipts, and a weighted average of sixes is six. The moment the weights stop
  adding to one you leave. That is what affine means: closed under weighted averages, and nothing
  more.
</Callout>

## Why Ax = 0 always gives a subspace

Nothing above depended on the numbers 1 and 2. Take any matrix $A$, and let $x$ and $y$ be two
vectors it sends to zero. Then

$$
A(x + y) = Ax + Ay = 0 + 0 = 0,
\qquad
A(cx) = c\,(Ax) = c\,0 = 0 .
$$

Both lines use only the property that $Ax$ distributes over sums and scalars. And $A0 = 0$ for
every matrix, so the zero vector is always in. The closure test passes, for every $A$ ever
written down.

This set has a name. For an $m$ by $n$ matrix $A$, the **null space** $N(A)$ is the set of all $x$
with $Ax = 0$. It is also called the kernel. Since $x$ has $n$ entries, $N(A)$ lives inside
$\R^n$, on the input side. The column space $C(A)$ from Lecture 7 lives inside $\R^m$, on the
output side. Two spaces in two different rooms, and mixing up which room is which is the most
common early mistake in this subject.

The solution set of $Ax = b$ for a nonzero $b$ gets no such name, because it is not a subspace. It
is an affine set, and Lecture 8 shows exactly which shift produces it.

## Which columns carry new information

Time to compute a null space instead of guessing one. Start from a small matrix and write it
twice, with the second copy doubled:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 8 & 6 & 16 \end{bmatrix} .
$$

Call this the doubled-columns matrix. Column 3 is twice column 1, and column 4 is twice column 2.
So only two of the four columns carry new information.

That redundancy is already a null vector in disguise. Column 3 minus twice column 1 is the zero
vector. Write down the recipe for that cancellation, one coefficient per column, and what you have
written down is a solution of $Ax = 0$.

A column of $R$ that holds a pivot is a **pivot column**, and its variable is determined. A column
with no pivot is a **free column**, and its variable can be set to any number you want. The count
of pivots is the **rank** $r$, so the count of free columns is $n - r$. Here $r = 2$ and $n = 4$,
so two of the four columns are free, and the null space has dimension two for exactly
that reason.

Elimination did not disturb the answer. Every row step is multiplication on the left by an
invertible matrix $E$. If $Ax = 0$ then $EAx = 0$, and if $EAx = 0$ then multiplying by $E^{-1}$
brings back $Ax = 0$. So

$$
N(A) = N(U) = N(R) .
$$

## The block machine

Suppose the pivot columns come first, so $R$ splits into an identity block beside a leftover
block:

$$
R = \begin{bmatrix} I & F \end{bmatrix},
$$

where $I$ is $r$ by $r$ and $F$ is $r$ by $n - r$. Split the unknown the same way. Write $y$ for
the pivot entries of $x$ and $z$ for the free entries. Then $Rx = 0$ reads

$$
I y + F z = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad y = -F z .
$$

That is the whole algorithm. Pick the free entries, and the pivot entries follow. The cleanest
picks are the columns of the identity, one free variable switched on at a time. Stack those
choices side by side and the answers stack with them:

$$
N = \begin{bmatrix} -F \\ I \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
R N = \begin{bmatrix} I & F \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -F \\ I \end{bmatrix} = -F + F = 0 .
$$

For the doubled-columns matrix the leftover block is
$F = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, so

$$
N = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
s_1 = (-2, 0, 1, 0), \qquad s_2 = (0, -2, 0, 1).
$$

These are the two vectors you guessed from looking at the columns. The columns of $N$ are called
the **special solutions**, one per free column, and every vector in $N(A)$ is a combination of
them. That is what makes them a basis, and it is why $\dim N(A) = n - r$.

import numpy as np

A = np.array([[1., 2., 2., 4.], [3., 8., 6., 16.]])
U = A.copy(); U[1] -= 3 * U[0]                  # one elimination step
R = U.copy(); R[1] /= 2; R[0] -= 2 * R[1]       # clear above the second pivot
print(R)                     # [[1. 0. 2. 0.]
                             #  [0. 1. 0. 2.]]

F = R[:, 2:]                                    # the free columns of R
N = np.vstack([-F, np.eye(2)]) + 0.0            # the block machine

## Where this is going

You can now answer $Ax = 0$ completely, for any matrix. Eliminate, find the pivots, put a 1 in
each free slot in turn, and read the pivot entries off with a flipped sign.

The harder question is the one this lecture opened with and then set aside. When $b$ is not zero
the solutions form an affine set, a shifted copy of the null space. Shifted by what? The answer is
short: find any single solution, then add the whole null space to it. That single solution is
called the particular solution, and the recipe is $x = x_p + x_n$. Lecture 8 builds it, checks
whether it exists at all, and sorts every matrix in the world into four cases by rank.
