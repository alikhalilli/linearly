## The idea in one sentence

When the columns of a matrix are perpendicular and have length 1, the expensive part of every
formula in this course disappears: the inverse becomes the transpose.

## The wish

Lecture 12 ended at a formula that works and costs too much. To fit a model to data that no
model fits exactly, you solve

$$
A\T A \,\hat{x} = A\T b .
$$

Everything about that line is fine except one thing. The matrix $A\T A$ has to be built and then
undone. Its entry in row $i$, column $j$ is the dot product of column $i$ of $A$ with column $j$.
So $A\T A$ is a table of how much each column of $A$ overlaps every other column. A good name for
it is the coupling matrix: the columns are tangled together, and solving means untangling them.

Now make a wish. What if $A\T A$ were the identity? Then $\hat{x} = A\T b$, and there is nothing
left to solve. One matrix multiplication and you are done.

The wish is really a request about the columns of $A$. Written out,
$A\T A = I$ says two things at once: every column has length 1, and every pair of different
columns has dot product 0. Perpendicular unit vectors. Nothing more.

So the rest of this lecture builds columns like that, and then spends them.

## Q, and the one identity that matters

Vectors $q_1, \ldots, q_n$ are **orthonormal** when

$$
q_i\T q_j = \begin{cases} 0 & i \neq j \\ 1 & i = j \end{cases}
$$

Put them in the columns of a matrix and the matrix gets the letter $Q$. Here is one, and every
entry is a third:

$$
Q = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & -2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Check the first column against itself: $(1 + 4 + 4)/9 = 1$. Check it against the second:
$(2 - 4 + 2)/9 = 0$. All nine checks work, and all nine checks are the entries of $Q\T Q$. Row $i$
of $Q\T$ is column $i$ of $Q$, so

$$
Q\T Q = I .
$$

That single identity is the whole lecture, and it asks for less than you might expect. $Q$ can be tall and
thin. If $Q$ is $5 \times 2$ with two orthonormal columns, $Q\T Q$ is the $2 \times 2$ identity,
and it still holds. What fails for a tall $Q$ is the other order: $QQ\T$ is $5 \times 5$ with
rank 2, so it cannot be $I_5$. You will meet that matrix again in a few paragraphs, doing useful
work.

When $Q$ is square, $Q\T Q = I$ makes $Q\T$ a genuine inverse, so $QQ\T = I$ too and

$$
Q^{-1} = Q\T .
$$

The expensive step in every formula so far becomes a transpose.

<Callout type="note">
  A naming trap worth stepping over now. Any matrix with orthonormal columns is called $Q$. Only
  the square ones get called **orthogonal matrices**. "Orthonormal matrix" would have been the
  honest name, and it is not the one in use. Say $Q$ and you are always safe.
</Callout>

## The orthogonal family

You have already dragged some of these. In Lecture 2 you moved four numbers around and watched
the plane move with them. Three of those machines were orthogonal, and now you can say why.

A **rotation** turns the plane by an angle $\theta$:

$$
Q = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Its columns are the landing spots of $e_1$ and $e_2$, both of length 1, and perpendicular because
they started perpendicular. At $\theta = 30°$ the columns are $(0.866, 0.5)$ and $(-0.5, 0.866)$.

A **reflection** flips the plane across a line. Across the line through the origin with direction
$(0.8, 0.6)$, the matrix is

$$
H = \begin{bmatrix} 0.28 & 0.96 \\ 0.96 & -0.28 \end{bmatrix},
$$

and $H\T H = I$ holds because $0.28^2 + 0.96^2 = 1$ and $0.28 (0.96) + 0.96(-0.28) = 0$. The next
section builds this matrix instead of quoting it.

A **permutation matrix** shuffles the entries of a vector. It has exactly one 1 in every row and
exactly one 1 in every column, and nothing else:

$$
P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
P\begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 7 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 9 \\ 5 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Its columns are the standard basis vectors in a new order, so they are already orthonormal, and
every permutation matrix is orthogonal. Reordering a list cannot change its length, which is the
same fact said in words.

## A mirror built from a shadow

Lecture 11 derived the shadow of one vector on another. For a **unit** vector $u$ the shadow of
$v$ is short:

$$
p = (u\T v)\, u .
$$

Now walk from $v$ to $p$, and keep walking the same distance again. You arrive on the far side of
the line through the origin perpendicular to $u$, at the same distance from it. That is a
reflection, and it is one subtraction:

$$
v' = v - 2(u\T v)\,u = \left(I - 2uu\T\right) v .
$$

The matrix $H = I - 2uu\T$ is the **Householder reflection**. It is symmetric, and applying it
twice returns you home, so $H\T H = H^2 = I$: orthogonal, with no work.

## Nothing stretches

Here is the property that makes numerical people reach for $Q$ before anything else. Take any
vector $x$ and hit it with $Q$. Its length is unchanged:

$$
\norm{Qx}^2 = (Qx)\T(Qx) = x\T Q\T Q x = x\T x = \norm{x}^2 .
$$

Two steps, and the middle one is the only fact in play. Angles survive too, because dot products
do:

$$
(Qx)\T(Qy) = x\T Q\T Q y = x\T y .
$$

Try it with the $Q$ from earlier. The vector $(1, -2, 3)$ has length $\sqrt{14} = 3.7416573\ldots$,
and so does $Q(1, -2, 3)$, to the last digit your computer keeps.

<Callout type="intuition">
  A general matrix can stretch some directions and crush others, and after a few hundred of them
  in a row the numbers in your machine are either enormous or gone. A $Q$ cannot do either. It
  turns the picture, or turns it over, and every length in it stays what it was. That is why
  stable code uses orthogonal matrices wherever it can.
</Callout>

## Projection, with the inverse deleted

Lecture 11 built the projection onto the column space of $A$:

$$
P = A(A\T A)^{-1} A\T .
$$

Set $A = Q$ and watch the middle collapse. Since $Q\T Q = I$, its inverse is $I$, and

$$
P = Q(Q\T Q)^{-1} Q\T = QQ\T .
$$

There is nothing to invert. Take the first two columns of the $Q$ above, call that tall matrix
$Q_2$, and project $b = (1,1,1)$ onto the plane they span:

$$
Q_2 Q_2\T\, b = \tfrac{1}{9}\begin{bmatrix} 5 & -2 & 4 \\ -2 & 8 & 2 \\ 4 & 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
= \tfrac{1}{9}\begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 8 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix} .
$$

Build $A(A\T A)^{-1}A\T$ from the same two columns before they were orthonormalized and you get
the identical matrix, after inverting a $2 \times 2$. Same answer, more work.

There is a second way to read $QQ\T b$. Multiply the other way round and it is a sum:

$$
QQ\T b = q_1 (q_1\T b) + q_2 (q_2\T b) + \cdots + q_n(q_n\T b).
$$

Each term is one shadow. So the projection is the sum of the shadows of $b$ on each $q$
separately, with no correction between them. That is what "no coupling" means, and it is exactly
what fails for columns that are not perpendicular: their shadows overlap, and the overlap is
$A\T A$.

## Wishful thinking: suppose $A = QR$

Real data does not arrive with orthonormal columns. So make a wish and follow it. Suppose the
matrix $A$ could be written as

$$
A = QR ,
$$

with $Q$ carrying orthonormal columns and $R$ carrying whatever is left over. Put that into the
normal equations and turn the handle:

$$
(QR)\T (QR)\,\hat{x} = (QR)\T b
$$

$$
R\T Q\T Q R\,\hat{x} = R\T Q\T b .
$$

The middle of the left side is $Q\T Q$, which is $I$, so it goes:

$$
R\T R\,\hat{x} = R\T Q\T b .
$$

Now $R\T$ sits in front of both sides. If $A$ has independent columns then $R$ is invertible, so
$R\T$ is too, and it cancels:

$$
R\,\hat{x} = Q\T b .
$$

Read what happened. The coupling matrix $A\T A$ is gone. In its place is one matrix multiplication
$Q\T b$ and a system with a triangular $R$, which back substitution solves top down in $n^2/2$
steps. No inverse is ever formed.

<Callout type="note">
  Two factorizations in this course use the letter $R$, and they are different matrices. In
  $A = CR$ from Lecture 6, $R$ is the row-reduced form that records which columns are independent.
  Here, in $A = QR$, $R$ is upper triangular and records the Gram-Schmidt coefficients. Same
  letter, no relation. When it matters, say "the triangular factor".
</Callout>

## Gram-Schmidt, on real numbers

Yes, and the recipe is one idea repeated: take the next column, subtract its shadows on
everything you have built so far, and divide by what is left.

Take three columns and run the whole thing. Nothing here is symbolic:

$$
a = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
b = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
c = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

**Step 1.** There is nothing to subtract from the first vector, so only normalize it.
$\norm{a} = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 4} = 3$, and

$$
q_1 = \tfrac{1}{3}(1, 2, 2).
$$

Record the length that came out: $r_{11} = 3$.

**Step 2.** Take $b$ and remove its shadow on $q_1$. The shadow coefficient is

$$
r_{12} = q_1\T b = \tfrac{1}{3}(1)(2) + \tfrac{1}{3}(2)(0) + \tfrac{1}{3}(2)(2) = \tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{4}{3} = 2 .
$$

Subtract:

$$
B = b - 2q_1 = (2, 0, 2) - \tfrac{2}{3}(1, 2, 2) = \left(\tfrac{4}{3}, -\tfrac{4}{3}, \tfrac{2}{3}\right).
$$

Check that this is perpendicular to $q_1$: $\tfrac{1}{3}\left(\tfrac{4}{3} - \tfrac{8}{3} +
\tfrac{4}{3}\right) = 0$. It is, by construction, because subtracting the shadow is exactly what
kills the overlap. Its length is $\tfrac{2}{3}\sqrt{4 + 4 + 1} = 2$, so $r_{22} = 2$ and

$$
q_2 = \tfrac{1}{3}(2, -2, 1).
$$

**Step 3.** Now $c$, with two shadows to remove:

Now collect the numbers you wrote down along the way. Each original vector is built from the
$q$'s using exactly the coefficients Gram-Schmidt produced:

$$
a = 3q_1, \qquad
b = 2q_1 + 2q_2, \qquad
c = \tfrac{5}{3}q_1 + \tfrac{1}{3}q_2 + \tfrac{1}{3}q_3 .
$$

Stack those coefficient lists as columns and you have $R$. Stack the $q$'s as columns and you have
$Q$:

$$
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{A}
=
\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & -2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}}_{Q}
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 & \tfrac{5}{3} \\ 0 & 2 & \tfrac{1}{3} \\ 0 & 0 & \tfrac{1}{3} \end{bmatrix}}_{R}.
$$

Multiply it out to check. Column 2 of the product is $2q_1 + 2q_2 = \tfrac{2}{3}(1,2,2) +
\tfrac{2}{3}(2,-2,1) = (2, 0, 2)$, which is $b$. The wish came true, and it came true for the
plain reason that every step of the process was a subtraction and a division.

Why is $R$ triangular? Because $q_2$ was born after $a$ was already finished. The vector $a$ is a
multiple of $q_1$ alone, so its column of $R$ has one entry and two zeros. In general
$R = Q\T A$, and the entry below the diagonal is $q_i\T a_j$ with $i > j$: a later $q$ against an
earlier $a$, and those are perpendicular.

import numpy as np

A = np.array([[1., 2., 1.],
              [2., 0., 1.],
              [2., 2., 1.]])         # columns a, b, c

qs, R = [], [[0.0] * 3 for _ in range(3)]
for j in range(3):
    v = A[:, j]
    for i in range(j):
        R[i][j] = float(qs[i] @ v)   # how much of q_i is still in v
        v = v - R[i][j] * qs[i]      # take the shadow away
    R[j][j] = float(np.linalg.norm(v))       # the length that is left
    qs.append(v / R[j][j])
Q = np.stack(qs, axis=1)

print(3 * Q)   # [[ 1.  2.  2.] [ 2. -2.  1.] [ 2.  1. -2.]]
print(R)       # [[3.0, 2.0, 1.6666666666666665],
               #  [0.0, 2.0, 0.33333333333333337],
               #  [0.0, 0.0, 0.3333333333333333]]
print(np.round(Q.T @ Q, 12))              # the identity
print(np.abs(Q @ np.array(R) - A).max())  # 0.0   exact here
x = np.array([1., -2., 3.])
print(np.linalg.norm(Q @ x))   # 3.741657386773942

## The same process, done stably

Written as an algorithm, Gram-Schmidt is six lines:

```text
for j = 1 to n
    v = column j of A
    for i = 1 to j-1
        R[i,j] = q_i dot v
        v      = v - R[i,j] * q_i
    R[j,j] = length of v
    q_j    = v / R[j,j]
```

Look closely at line 4. The dot product is taken against `v`, the vector as it stands right now,
with earlier shadows already removed. The classical version takes it against the original
column instead: `R[i,j] = q_i dot A[:,j]`. In exact arithmetic the two agree, since the pieces
already removed were perpendicular to $q_i$ anyway. In floating point they do not, because the
first version measures the small leftover directly while the second measures a big vector and
hopes the large parts cancel.

The version above is called **modified Gram-Schmidt**, and the difference is not academic. Run
both on a matrix whose columns nearly coincide and check how far $Q\T Q$ drifts from the identity:

```python
import numpy as np

def gram_schmidt(A, classical):
    qs = []
    for j in range(A.shape[1]):
        v = A[:, j].copy()
        for i in range(j):
            r = qs[i] @ (A[:, j] if classical else v)   # the line that differs
            v = v - r * qs[i]
        qs.append(v / np.linalg.norm(v))
    return np.stack(qs, axis=1)

eps = 1e-8
A = np.array([[1., 1., 1.],
              [eps, 0., 0.],
              [0., eps, 0.],
              [0., 0., eps]])         # three columns that almost agree

for name, Q in [("classical", gram_schmidt(A, True)),
                ("modified ", gram_schmidt(A, False)),
                ("library  ", np.linalg.qr(A)[0])]:
    print(name, f"{np.abs(Q.T @ Q - np.eye(3)).max():.3e}")
# classical 5.000e-01     the columns are not perpendicular at all
# modified  7.071e-09     eight digits kept
# library   6.661e-16     Householder mirrors, full precision
```

Half. The classical version returns columns that are not remotely perpendicular, and it does so
silently. Modified Gram-Schmidt keeps eight digits. Libraries do better still by never building
$Q$ this way at all: they apply a chain of Householder mirrors, each one an exact orthogonal
matrix, and lengths cannot drift when nothing stretches.

## Two roads to least squares

Now the payoff, priced honestly. You have two ways to solve a least squares problem.

The first road builds $A\T A$ and solves. The second road factors $A = QR$ and solves
$R\hat{x} = Q\T b$ by back substitution. On clean data they agree to the last digit. Take the
measurements table from Lecture 12, put a column of ones beside the two feature columns, and both
roads return $\hat{x} = (14.855866, -2.079330, -0.541899)$.

The roads part when the columns of $A$ nearly line up, which is what real features do. Here is a
matrix built to lean that way, with a knob $d$ that squeezes two columns together:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1+d \\ 1 & 1-d \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The **condition number** of a matrix is the ratio of its largest stretch to its smallest, and it
predicts how many digits a solve can lose. For this $A$ it grows like $1/d$. For $A\T A$ it grows
like $1/d^2$, because forming $A\T A$ squares every stretch factor. Squaring a condition number
means throwing away half your digits before you start.

import numpy as np

def make(d):
    A = np.array([[1., 1.], [1., 1. + d], [1., 1. - d]])
    return A, A @ np.array([1., 1.])   # so the answer is exactly (1, 1)

for d in [1e-2, 1e-6, 1e-8]:
    A, b = make(d)
    s = np.linalg.svd(A, compute_uv=False)
    t = np.linalg.svd(A.T @ A, compute_uv=False)
    print(d, s[0] / s[-1], t[0] / t[-1])
# 0.01  2.45e+02  6.00e+04
# 1e-06 2.45e+06  6.00e+12
# 1e-08 2.45e+08  8.83e+16   squaring the condition costs half the digits

A, b = make(1e-6)
print(np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A, A.T @ b))   # [1.00022207 0.99977793]
Q, R = np.linalg.qr(A)
print(np.linalg.solve(R, Q.T @ b))         # [1. 1.]        exact

A, b = make(1e-8)
print(np.linalg.det(A.T @ A))       # 0.0   AtA is now singular
Q, R = np.linalg.qr(A)
print(np.linalg.solve(R, Q.T @ b))  # [1.00000001 0.99999999]   QR is fine

At $d = 10^{-6}$ the normal equations lose four digits and QR loses none. At $d = 10^{-8}$ the
normal equations stop working: all four entries of $A\T A$ round to the same number 3, its
determinant is exactly zero, and the solver refuses. The matrix $A$ itself is untouched. It still
has two independent columns and an ordinary condition number. All the damage was done by the step
that formed $A\T A$, and QR never takes that step.

<Callout type="intuition">
  This is why `np.linalg.lstsq` does not use the normal equations, and why no serious library
  does. The formula $\hat{x} = (A\T A)^{-1}A\T b$ is the right way to understand least squares and
  the wrong way to compute it. Understand it with the normal equations; compute it with $QR$.
</Callout>

## Why deep networks are initialised with Q

A deep network multiplies its input by one matrix per layer. Sixty layers is sixty
multiplications, and whatever length the signal has at the end is whatever those sixty matrices
left of it. The callout above claimed an orthogonal matrix can do neither harm. Here is that
claim run for six steps and measured.

Start from $x = (1, 0)$ and multiply repeatedly by three different matrices. The first is the
rotation $Q$ with columns $(0.6, 0.8)$ and $(-0.8, 0.6)$. The other two are ordinary matrices
with no special structure.

Six steps already spread the two ordinary matrices by a factor of nearly $200$. At sixty layers
the same arithmetic gives numbers your machine cannot hold at one end and cannot distinguish
from zero at the other. That is the exploding and vanishing signal problem, and it is a fact
about $\norm{Ax}$ before it is a fact about training.

This is why orthogonal initialisation exists. Give every layer a $Q$ and the signal arrives at
the last layer the same length it left the first, whatever the depth. The gradient runs the
same gauntlet backwards through the transposes, and $Q\T$ is orthogonal too, so it is protected
by the identical two-line proof.

## Where this is going

You now have a factorization that turns any independent columns into perpendicular unit ones, and
a reason to want it. Two things follow.

The first is that $Q$ shows up again as the answer to a different question. Lecture 15 asks which
directions a matrix leaves alone, and for symmetric matrices the answer is a full orthonormal set:
$S = Q\Lambda Q\T$, a rotation, a stretch along the axes, and the rotation undone. Every fact in
this lecture is waiting there.

The second is that Gram-Schmidt needs independent columns, and dies when they are not. When two
columns coincide exactly, $r_{22}$ comes out zero and you divide by it. The tool that handles rank
honestly, and handles nearly-dependent columns better than anything here, is the singular value
decomposition, which Part VI reaches by combining this lecture with the next two.

The third is far away and worth a bookmark. The promise $Q$ makes, lengths kept exactly, becomes
an engineering guarantee in [Lecture 29](/lectures/lecture-29): a whole family of fine-tuning
methods updates a trained network by rotating its weights, chosen precisely because an orthogonal
matrix cannot blow up or crush anything it touches. The mirrors $I - 2uu^{\T}$ from this
lecture's engine room reappear there with their normals in training.
