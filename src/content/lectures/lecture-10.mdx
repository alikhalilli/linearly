## The idea in one sentence

The four subspaces come in two perpendicular pairs, and each pair covers its whole space with
nothing left over, which is why every reachable $b$ comes from one and only one $x$.

## The matrix and its four bases

Everything in this chapter happens inside one $3 \times 4$ matrix, the same one Lecture 9 took
apart. Its four bases are already in hand:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 8 & 6 & 16 \\ 4 & 10 & 8 & 20 \end{bmatrix},
\qquad r = 2 .
$$

In $\R^4$: the row space $C(A\T)$ has basis $(1, 0, 2, 0)$ and $(0, 1, 0, 2)$, and the null
space $N(A)$ has basis $s_1 = (-2, 0, 1, 0)$ and $s_2 = (0, -2, 0, 1)$.

In $\R^3$: the column space $C(A)$ has basis $(1, 3, 4)$ and $(2, 8, 10)$, and the left null
space $N(A\T)$ has basis $(-1, -1, 1)$.

Four sets of vectors, four dimensions that add to 4 and to 3. What the last chapter did not say
is how they sit relative to each other. That is one line of algebra away.

## The proof is one line

Take any $x$ in the null space. The statement $Ax = 0$ looks like a fact about a matrix. Read it
by rows and it is a fact about angles. Each entry of $Ax$ is a row of $A$ dotted with $x$, so
$Ax = 0$ says every one of those dot products is zero:

$$
\text{row}_1 \cdot x = 0, \qquad \text{row}_2 \cdot x = 0, \qquad \text{row}_3 \cdot x = 0 .
$$

A zero dot product means a right angle. So $x$ is perpendicular to every row of $A$. It is then
perpendicular to every combination of the rows, because dotting distributes over sums and scalar
multiples. The combinations of the rows are the row space. Done:

$$
N(A) \perp C(A\T) .
$$

Now turn the whole argument around by writing $A\T$ where $A$ was. Take any $y$ in the left null
space. Then $A\T y = 0$ says every row of $A\T$, which is to say every column of $A$, is
perpendicular to $y$. So $y$ is perpendicular to every combination of the columns:

$$
N(A\T) \perp C(A) .
$$

Check it on the numbers. The left null space vector is $(-1, -1, 1)$, and the two column space
basis vectors give $-1 - 3 + 4 = 0$ and $-2 - 8 + 10 = 0$. Both zero, both right angles.

Two subspaces are called orthogonal when every vector in one is perpendicular to every vector in
the other. That is a strong condition, and it has an immediate consequence. If some vector $v$
belonged to both, it would have to be perpendicular to itself, so $v \cdot v = 0$, so $v = 0$.
Orthogonal subspaces meet at the origin and nowhere else.

## The right angle you can draw

Abstract right angles are easy to nod at and hard to feel. Here is one with numbers. Take
$x = (1, 1, 1, 1)$ and split it into the row space piece and the null space piece from Lecture 9:

$$
x_r = \left(\tfrac{3}{5}, \tfrac{3}{5}, \tfrac{6}{5}, \tfrac{6}{5}\right), \qquad
x_n = \left(\tfrac{2}{5}, \tfrac{2}{5}, -\tfrac{1}{5}, -\tfrac{1}{5}\right).
$$

Their dot product is $\tfrac{6}{25} + \tfrac{6}{25} - \tfrac{6}{25} - \tfrac{6}{25} = 0$. So the
two pieces of $x$ sit at a right angle, and the three vectors $x_r$, $x_n$ and $x$ form a right
triangle inside $\R^4$. That triangle lives in an ordinary flat plane, so you can draw it exactly,
and Pythagoras has to hold:

$$
\norm{x_r}^2 + \norm{x_n}^2 \;=\; 3.6 + 0.4 \;=\; 4 \;=\; \norm{x}^2 .
$$

The four subspaces do better than that. Nothing is left over, and the proof runs both ways in two
short steps.

If $x$ is in $N(A)$ then $Ax = 0$, so $x$ is perpendicular to every row, so $x$ is perpendicular
to the row space. That is the direction already proved. Now the other one. Suppose $x$ is
perpendicular to every row of $A$. Then every entry of $Ax$, being a row dotted with $x$, is zero,
so $Ax = 0$ and $x$ is in $N(A)$.

Put those together and the null space holds every vector perpendicular to the row space, and
nothing else. The name for that is the orthogonal complement, and the dimensions are the receipt:

$$
r + (n - r) = n, \qquad r + (m - r) = m .
$$

For our matrix, $2 + 2 = 4$ in $\R^4$ and $2 + 1 = 3$ in $\R^3$. If the null space had come out
one dimension short, some direction perpendicular to the row space would have had nowhere to
live, and the count would have caught it.

The $\R^3$ side is small enough to draw honestly. The column space is a plane through the origin.
The left null space is the single line through $(-1, -1, 1)$. The plane and the line are
perpendicular, and a plane plus its normal line is all of $\R^3$.

## Every reachable b comes from exactly one x

Here is the payoff.

Take any $b$ in the column space. Since $b$ is a combination of columns, there is some $x$ with
$Ax = b$. Split it: $x = x_r + x_n$, and $Ax_n = 0$, so $Ax_r = b$ as well. So $b$ comes from at
least one vector in the row space.

Could it come from two? Suppose $x_r$ and $x_r'$ both sit in the row space and both satisfy
$Ax_r = Ax_r' = b$. Then $A(x_r - x_r') = 0$, so the difference is in the null space. It is also
in the row space, since the row space is closed under subtraction. A vector in both spaces is
perpendicular to itself, and the only such vector is zero. So $x_r = x_r'$.

$$
\text{every } b \in C(A) \text{ comes from one and only one } x_r \in C(A\T).
$$

The matrix may crush a whole two-dimensional null space to zero. Underneath the crushing there is
still a perfect one-to-one correspondence between two spaces of dimension $r$, and $A$ restricted
to the row space is invertible.

Watch it happen. The vectors $x = (1, 1, 1, 1)$ and $x' = (-1, 1, 2, 1)$ differ by the null space
vector $s_1$, so both give the same output:

$$
Ax = Ax' = (9, 33, 42).
$$

Feed each through the split and both hand back the same row space vector,
$x_r = \left(\tfrac{3}{5}, \tfrac{3}{5}, \tfrac{6}{5}, \tfrac{6}{5}\right)$. Two inputs, one
output, one row space representative. And that representative is the shortest of all the
solutions, since $\norm{x_r} \approx 1.897$ while $\norm{x} = 2$ and $\norm{x'} \approx 2.646$.
Pythagoras again: any other solution carries an extra perpendicular leg, and a leg can only make
a vector longer.

Read the panels off the two null spaces. A null space that is only the zero vector means no two
inputs collide, so a solution is unique when it exists. A left null space that is only the zero
vector means the column space fills $\R^m$, so every $b$ is reachable. Case 1 has both, which is
what invertible means. Case 4 has neither, and every unpleasant thing that can happen to a linear
system happens there.

The part that survives all four cases is the one-to-one link across the middle. Even in case 4,
where $A$ crushes a null space and misses part of $\R^m$, the row space and the column space are
still perfectly matched.

## The pseudoinverse, defined honestly

A matrix with a nonzero null space has no inverse. There is no undoing the crush: once $s_1$ and
$0$ have both been sent to $0$, nothing downstream can tell them apart. That looks like the end
of the story. The one-to-one link says otherwise.

Here is what $A^{+}$, the pseudoinverse, does with a vector $b$ in $\R^m$.

1. Split $b$ into the part inside $C(A)$ and the part inside $N(A\T)$. Those two spaces are
   complements, so the split exists and is unique.
2. Throw away the $N(A\T)$ part. It was never reachable, so no $x$ was ever going to produce it.
3. Undo the one-to-one link on what is left, landing on the single $x_r$ in the row space with
   $A x_r$ equal to the kept part.

In one line: invert the part that is invertible and ignore the null spaces. Every property of
$A^{+}$ follows from those three steps. $A^{+}A$ is the projection onto the row space, because it
sends $x$ to $x_r$. $AA^{+}$ is the projection onto the column space, because it sends $b$ to its
reachable part. And $AA^{+}A = A$, since $A$ ignores the discarded pieces anyway.

  The pseudoinverse is the return trip along the one-to-one link: blue out, green back. It reaches only the row space,
  and it listens only to the column space. The two faded rooms are exactly the information $A$
  destroyed, and no inverse of any kind can bring them back.
</Figure>

On the running matrix, `numpy.linalg.pinv` reproduces all of this exactly. Feeding it
$b = (9, 33, 42)$ returns $\left(\tfrac{3}{5}, \tfrac{3}{5}, \tfrac{6}{5}, \tfrac{6}{5}\right)$,
the row space representative. Feed it the unreachable $b' = (8, 32, 43)$, which is $b$ plus the
left null space vector, and it returns the same answer, because step 2 discarded the difference.

```python
import numpy as np
np.set_printoptions(precision=4, suppress=True)

A = np.array([[1., 2., 2., 4.], [3., 8., 6., 16.], [4., 10., 8., 20.]])
Aplus = np.linalg.pinv(A)

b = np.array([9., 33., 42.])          # reachable: b is in C(A)
print(Aplus @ b)                      # [0.6 0.6 1.2 1.2]   the row space x
print(A @ (Aplus @ b))                # [ 9. 33. 42.]       reached exactly

x = np.array([1., 1., 1., 1.])
s1 = np.array([-2., 0., 1., 0.])
print(Aplus @ A @ x)                  # [0.6 0.6 1.2 1.2]   x projected onto the row space
print(Aplus @ A @ s1)                 # [0. 0. 0. 0.]       the null part dies

b_off = np.array([8., 32., 43.])      # b plus the left null space vector
print(Aplus @ b_off)                  # [0.6 0.6 1.2 1.2]   same answer
print(A @ (Aplus @ b_off) - b_off)    # [ 1.  1. -1.]  the part it threw away
print(np.allclose(A @ Aplus @ A, A))  # True
```

  value decomposition, which builds orthonormal bases for all four subspaces at once and inverts
  only the nonzero singular values. That is steps 1 through 3 above, done numerically. Part VI
  constructs it. What you have here is the shape of the answer.

## Where this is going

Two things in this chapter were used before they were earned. The split $x = x_r + x_n$ was
described as though computing it were easy, and step 1 of the pseudoinverse asked for the part of
$b$ that lies inside the column space. Both are the same operation: dropping a vector
perpendicularly onto a subspace. That operation is called projection, it has a short formula, and
it is the next chapter. Once you can project, the whole of least squares is one substitution away,
and least squares is how every linear model in machine learning gets fitted.
