## The idea in one sentence

A vector space is any collection where you can add and scale without ever falling out, and once
you know what to look for you find them everywhere, including inside the columns of your data.

## The rules that make adding worth the name

Every result so far has come from two moves. Scale a thing. Add two things. Lecture 1 called the
combination of those moves a linear combination and never asked what "thing" meant. It never had
to. But at some point you have to say what you are assuming about addition, because the whole
subject rests on it.

Start with what addition must never do. It must not care about the order you write things in, and
it must not care how you group them. If $u + v$ and $v + u$ could differ, or if
$(u + v) + w$ and $u + (v + w)$ could differ, then a sum of three vectors would not be a single
object and the phrase "the sum" would be meaningless.

Next, addition needs a place to stand still. There has to be one element, called $0$, that changes
nothing when you add it. And every move has to be undoable: for each $u$ there is a $-u$ that
brings you back to $0$. Without those two you could walk but never return, and an algebra you
cannot run backwards is not much use.

Now scaling. Scaling twice should be the same as scaling once by the product, so $a(bu) = (ab)u$.
Scaling has to distribute over adding, in both senses: scaling a sum is the same as scaling the
parts, $a(u + v) = au + av$, and adding two scale factors is the same as scaling twice and adding,
$(a + b)u = au + bu$. These two are what tie the two operations together. Drop them, and scaling and
adding go their separate ways.

The last rule looks too obvious to state. Scaling by 1 must do nothing:

$$
1\,u = u .
$$

It is not obvious and it is not free. Take $\R^2$ with ordinary addition, but define $c\,(x, y)$ to
be $(cx, 0)$ for every scalar. Check the other seven rules and they all hold. But $1(0,1) = (0,0)$,
so the last rule fails, and the object it produces is useless: every vector is scaled into the
horizontal axis and information disappears for no reason. That eighth rule is what stops scaling
from quietly destroying the space.

Those are the eight rules, and together they are the definition. A vector space is a set with an
addition and a scalar multiplication that obey them. Nothing in the list mentions arrows or
coordinates.

<Callout type="note">
  The rules say nothing about angle or distance. You cannot ask whether two vectors are
  perpendicular using only these eight, because perpendicular needs a dot product, and a dot
  product is extra structure sitting on top. Part IV of this course adds it. Until then, everything
  is done with adding and scaling alone.
</Callout>

## Three spaces that do not look like arrows

Since the rules never mention arrows, other things qualify. Here are three, and each one is worth
holding in your head, because they are the reason the abstraction earns its keep.

**Two-by-two matrices.** Call the set $M$. Add two of them entry by entry, scale one by
multiplying every entry. Both operations stay inside $M$: a $2\times 2$ matrix plus a $2\times 2$
matrix is a $2\times 2$ matrix. Watch a real combination:

$$
2\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}
+ \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 7 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The zero vector of this space is the zero matrix, and the additive inverse of a matrix is the
matrix with every sign flipped. The scaling identity reads

$$
1 \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix},
$$

which is scaling, and has nothing to do with the matrix of all ones. Notice that naming an element
of $M$ takes four numbers, exactly as naming a point of $\R^4$ does. That resemblance is real, and
Lecture 8 gives it a name.

**Functions.** Call the set $F$: every real function $f(x)$. Adding two functions means adding
their values at each point, $(f + g)(x) = f(x) + g(x)$. Scaling means multiplying every value.
Take $f(x) = x^2$ and $g(x) = 1 - x/2$. Then $(f+g)(x) = x^2 - x/2 + 1$, which at $x = -2$ is
$4 + 1 + 1 = 6$. The zero vector is the function that is zero everywhere. Nothing here has
coordinates. To name one element of $F$ you have to give a number for every real input, so this
space is not $\R^n$ for any $n$. It is where the mathematics behind kernel methods and Fourier
analysis lives.

**The zero space.** Call it $Z$, and let it contain the single element $0$. Adding gives
$0 + 0 = 0$, staying inside. Scaling gives $c\,0 = 0$, staying inside. Every one of the eight rules
holds, dully. $Z$ is a vector space. The empty set is not, because a vector space has to contain a
zero, and the empty set contains nothing at all. The smallest possible vector space has one
element, and it will keep turning up.

## Subspaces: two questions, and that is all

Whole vector spaces are useful. The pieces inside them matter more. A subspace
of a vector space $V$ is a subset that is a vector space in its own right, using the same addition
and the same scaling.

Checking eight rules on every candidate would be miserable, and it is unnecessary. Six of the eight
say things like "addition does not care about order", and if that is true for all of $V$ it is
automatically true for anything sitting inside $V$. Only one thing can go wrong: a sum or a
scaling can walk out of the subset. So the whole test is two questions.

Take any $v$ and $w$ in the subset $S$, and any scalar $c$:

1. Is $v + w$ still in $S$?
2. Is $c\,v$ still in $S$?

If both answers are yes for every choice, $S$ is a subspace. That property has a name, closure, and
that is the entire definition.

One consequence comes for free. Put $c = 0$ in the second question. Then $0\,v$ is in $S$, and
$0\,v$ is the zero vector. Every subspace contains the zero vector, so a plane in $\R^3$ that
misses the origin cannot be a subspace, and you can rule such a set out at a glance.

Now the instructive failure. Take the first quadrant of the plane, all points with both
coordinates at least zero. It looks well behaved. Test it.

Add $v = (2, 3)$ and $w = (3, 1)$. The sum is $(5, 4)$, still in the quadrant. Try other pairs and
the same thing happens, because adding two non-negative numbers never gives a negative one. The
first question passes, every time.

Now scale. Take $c = -1$:

$$
-1\,(2, 3) = (-2, -3),
$$

which is in the opposite corner of the plane. The second question fails on the first try. The
first quadrant is not a subspace, and it fails for a clean reason: a set can be perfectly
closed under addition and still be broken, because going backwards is part of what scaling means.

## Every span is a subspace

Lecture 1 defined the span of a set of vectors as everything you can reach by combining them. That
was a picture. Now it is a theorem, and the proof takes two lines.

Let $S$ be the span of $v$ and $w$, so a typical element is $a v + b w$. Add two of them:

$$
(a_1 v + b_1 w) + (a_2 v + b_2 w) = (a_1 + a_2) v + (b_1 + b_2) w,
$$

which is again a combination of $v$ and $w$, so it is in $S$. Scale one of them:

$$
c\,(a v + b w) = (ca) v + (cb) w,
$$

also a combination, also in $S$. Both questions answered. The span is a subspace, and the same two
lines work for any number of vectors.

This is why the gallery looks the way it does. The span of one nonzero vector in $\R^3$ is a line
through the origin. The span of two vectors pointing in different directions is a plane through the
origin, and Lecture 1 met one: the combinations of $(1,1,1)$ and $(2,3,4)$ fill a plane, and
$(1,0,0)$ is off it. Spans always run through the origin, because taking all the coefficients zero
is allowed.

## The column space of a matrix

Now put the pieces together, and the payoff arrives.

Here is a ratings table. Five people, three films, each entry a score out of ten:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 4 & 8 \\ 2 & 8 & 7 \\ 1 & 5 & 9 \\ 4 & 7 & 2 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Read it by columns, the way Lecture 2 taught. Column 1 is film 1, described by the five scores it
received: $m_1 = (5, 3, 2, 1, 4)$. Column 2 is film 2 and column 3 is film 3. Each film is a vector
in $\R^5$, one coordinate per viewer.

Mixing the films means combining the columns. Take two parts of film 1, one part of film 2 and
none of film 3:

$$
2 m_1 + 1 m_2 + 0 m_3 = (12, 10, 12, 7, 15),
$$

and that is $Ax$ with $x = (2, 1, 0)$. It is a prediction: if a new film were exactly this blend,
those five numbers are what the five viewers would give it.

Now ask the question this course keeps asking. As $x$ ranges over all of $\R^3$, what can $Ax$
be? The answer is the span of the three columns. It is a subspace of $\R^5$, by the theorem in the
last section, and it has a name and a symbol:

$$
C(A) \;=\; \{\, Ax : x \in \R^3 \,\}.
$$

This is the column space. It contains a lot more than the three columns themselves. It contains
every blend of them, and the columns are just three of its members.

## Why the ambient space matters

$C(A)$ lives inside $\R^5$, and it is a small part of it. Three columns can span at most three
dimensions, so $C(A)$ is at most a three-dimensional slab inside a five-dimensional room. Almost
nothing in $\R^5$ is in it.

That smallness is the shape of the problem. Take the reachable vector
$b = (12, 10, 12, 7, 15)$ and change one entry by one, to $b' = (12, 10, 12, 7, 16)$. Nothing about
$b'$ looks unusual. But no weights on the three films produce it. The closest the column space can
get misses by

$$
(0.1712,\; -0.1117,\; 0.3497,\; -0.1715,\; -0.2622)
$$

and that miss has squared length $0.2622$. One viewer rated the blend one point higher than the
films can explain, and the whole vector fell out of the space.

That gap is the story of everything that follows. It is why no real ratings matrix is reproduced
exactly by a handful of factors, and why "solve $Ax = b$" usually turns into "get as close to $b$
as $C(A)$ allows". Part IV of this course does that properly and calls it least squares.

<Callout type="intuition">
  Two spaces, two different rooms. The weights $x$ live in $\R^3$, one number per film. The
  predictions $Ax$ live in $\R^5$, one number per viewer. A matrix carries vectors from one room
  to the other, and $C(A)$ is the part of the second room it can actually reach. Whenever a matrix
  confuses you, ask which room each vector is standing in.
</Callout>

import numpy as np

A = np.array([[5., 2, 3], [3, 4, 8], [2, 8, 7], [1, 5, 9], [4, 7, 2]])
print(A.shape, np.linalg.matrix_rank(A))  # (5, 3) 3   a slab inside R^5

x = np.array([2., 1, 0])
b = A @ x
print(b)  # [12. 10. 12.  7. 15.]  a blend

# ask for the best weights, see what is left over
print((np.linalg.pinv(A) @ b).round(6))  # [2. 1. 0.]  reached exactly

b2 = np.array([12., 10, 12, 7, 16])  # one score, one point higher
best = np.linalg.pinv(A) @ b2
print((A @ best - b2).round(4))    # [ 0.1712 -0.1117  0.3497 -0.1715 -0.2622]
print(np.sum((A @ best - b2) ** 2))  # 0.2622   b2 is outside C(A)

## Where this is going

Adding and scaling turned out to define everything, and the sets that survive both moves are the
subspaces. The columns of a matrix span one of them, $C(A)$, and asking whether $Ax = b$ has a
solution is asking whether $b$ lies in it.

That is half of the picture, and it is the half about outputs. The other half is about inputs.
Some $x$ get sent to zero: the ring road machine from Lecture 2 flattened $(1,1,1)$ to nothing, and
a matrix with a repeated column will flatten something too. Those blind directions form a subspace
of their own, sitting in the room where $x$ lives. It is called the null space, and finding it is
where the next chapter starts.
